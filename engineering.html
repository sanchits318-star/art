Engineering & Building 🛠️🏗️

Let's design, build, test, crash and build again!

✨ Why Engineering Matters ✨

Engineering allows children to explore how things are built, how they work, and how they move. Through constructing bridges, cars, towers, and other structures, children develop problem-solving skills, curiosity, and creativity. In early childhood education, engineering activities encourage experimentation, planning, and persistence. Children learn that there are many ways to solve a problem — every design, structure, and creation can be improved or reimagined. These experiences support logical thinking, collaboration, and resilience while fostering creativity across all curriculum areas.

🧠 Creativity Theories 🧠

The Reggio Emilia approach (Malaguzzi, 1996) encourages children to learn through making and doing, valuing multiple ways of exploring and expressing ideas. Vygotsky (1978) emphasised that creativity develops through social interaction and guided play, which aligns with collaborative engineering tasks. Gardner’s theory of multiple intelligences recognises bodily-kinesthetic and logical-mathematical abilities, supporting children’s individual strengths in designing, building, and testing objects. Engineering activities promote problem-solving, reflection, and trial-and-error learning, helping children develop both cognitive and practical skills.

💭 Reflection 💭

Reflecting on my engineering videos, I feel proud of how well certain aspects went. In the “Build a Strong Bridge” activity I successfully engaged children by encouraging hands-on problem-solving. I noticed they were excited to test their bridges and quickly understood how folding paper made the structure stronger. The questioning I used such as asking what worked well and what could be improved, helped them think critically and make design decisions.

In the “Build a Rolling Car” activity, children were motivated to experiment with motion and speed. They learned through trial and error testing wheels, adjusting weight and observing how different forces affected movement. I enjoyed seeing their creativity as they decorated and personalized their cars, demonstrating ownership of their designs.

There were areas for improvement. Some children needed more guidance in constructing stable bridges or aligning wheels correctly. I also realized that explaining engineering concepts more clearly with visual examples beforehand could have supported understanding. In another such lesson I would introduce a short demonstration first to model key techniques, then allow extended time for testing and reflection. I would also ask more open-ended questions during the building process to prompt deeper thinking. These strategies would enhance creativity, critical thinking, and problem-solving skills, helping children gain confidence as little engineers.

✨ Why Technologies Matter ✨

Technologies introduce children to tools, devices, and digital resources that help them explore, create, and solve problems. By using simple machines, computers, tablets, and coding toys, children develop creativity, critical thinking, and confidence in experimenting with technology. Early exposure to technology encourages children to ask questions, design solutions, and test ideas safely. These experiences help children understand cause and effect, develop planning and problem-solving skills, and express creativity in new ways across all curriculum areas.

🧠 Creativity Theories 🧠

The Reggio Emilia approach (Malaguzzi, 1996) values technology as a medium for children to express ideas and document learning. Vygotsky (1978) emphasised learning through guided interaction and collaborative problem-solving, which aligns with using technology in group activities. Gardner’s theory of multiple intelligences highlights logical-mathematical and visual-spatial skills, supporting creativity in programming, building, and designing with technology. Activities involving technology foster experimentation, reflection, and iterative problem-solving, helping children develop both cognitive and technical skills.

🌟 Reflection 🌟

Reflecting on my engineering videos, I feel proud of how well certain aspects went. In the “Build a Strong Bridge” activity, I was able to engage children through hands-on problem-solving. I noticed how excited they were to test their bridges and how quickly they realised that folding the paper made the structures stronger. By asking questions like what worked well and what could be improved, I helped them think critically and make meaningful design decisions.

In the “Build a Rolling Car” activity, I saw how motivated the children were to experiment with motion and speed. They learned through trial and error by changing wheels, adjusting weight, and observing how different forces affected their cars. I enjoyed watching their creativity as they personalised and decorated their cars, showing ownership of their creations.

I also recognised some areas that could be improved. Some children needed more guidance when building stable bridges or fixing their wheels. I realised that introducing the engineering concepts more clearly with visual examples at the start would have supported their understanding. If I were to repeat this lesson, I would begin with a short demonstration to model key techniques and then provide more time for testing and reflecting. I would also include more open-ended questions throughout the process to prompt deeper thinking. These changes would help strengthen their creativity, critical thinking, and confidence as young engineers.
